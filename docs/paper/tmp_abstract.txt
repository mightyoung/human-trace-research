Cross-camera re-identification is reported above 90% mAP, yet that number does not tell an integrator which identity functions a network supports. We treat this as a measurement problem in identity science, answered by a deployment protocol. From a six-camera office floor (1,574 tracks, 443 hypothesised identities), an eight-segment corpus with 26 named identities, and three further capture days, we reduce availability to three probes measurable in one working day, each gating one tier: face pixel budget, regional co-visibility, wardrobe diversity. Its five tiers each carry a measured benefit: same-camera identity across time (42.3% recall); cross-camera identity inside a co-visible region (AUC 0.904 frozen OSNet, 0.994 independent encoder); an attribute-consistency gate that raises merge precision from 0.730 to 0.930 at unchanged recall; pose-conditioned templates that raise selector recall from 56.4% to 68.2% with no face at all, and to 69.8% with a measured face channel against an 83.2% identity-oracle ceiling; and a 27-item queue resolvable in 20-30 minutes. Outside that pair, cross-camera appearance sits at chance across six encoder and alignment configurations (AUC 0.450 to 0.622, every interval covering 0.5), and the face channel is unusable below 60 px (AUC 0.352 against 0.847). The tiers cannot be collapsed: a local constraint on a global partition damages correct merges it never addressed, and a verifier with pairwise AUC 0.833 destroys three of five known-correct merges. Re-run across capture days, coverage moves by 0.001 while merge error doubles, and the best same-day gallery transfers worst. The binding constraint is capture geometry, not the encoder.
